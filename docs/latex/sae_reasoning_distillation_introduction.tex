\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Reasoning traces expose intermediate steps that can help language models solve complex tasks~\citep{wei2022chain}.
They also provide supervision for transferring capabilities from larger teachers to smaller students~\citep{hsieh2023distilling}.
Yet a trace that leads a capable teacher to the correct answer need not be an effective teaching example for a smaller model.
Two correct solutions may differ in their explanations, intermediate calculations, verification steps, and repeated statements.
Understanding which of these differences matter for learning is essential to improving the quality and cost of reasoning distillation.

Our starting point is a controlled observation: with the teacher, student, and training questions fixed, shorter correct teacher traces can train a more accurate student than longer correct traces.
Preliminary experiments on GSM8K~\citep{cobbe2021training} exhibit this pattern when correct candidates are ranked by their generated length.
Because the alternatives solve the same problems and reach the correct final answers, the comparison motivates examining the structure of the supervision itself.
One possible explanation is that some of the additional content in long traces is redundant for the student, or makes the relevant solution steps harder to learn.
Length alone, however, does not identify that content, and differences in supervision volume and loss weighting provide alternative explanations.
We therefore treat redundancy as a hypothesis to investigate through representation analysis and intervention.

This motivates two questions.
\emph{What latent features distinguish short and long correct reasoning traces generated by the same model for the same problem?}
\emph{Can manipulating those features produce traces that are more effective for training a smaller student?}
Together, these questions connect an observable difference in generated text to a potential mechanism for improving the teacher's data distribution.
The desired outcome is a complete, correct solution that uses its reasoning budget effectively and retains useful supervision for the student.

We propose a framework based on sparse autoencoders (SAEs), which decompose model activations into sparsely active learned directions~\citep{cunningham2023sparse}.
A shared TopK SAE~\citep{gao2024scaling} is trained on residual-stream activations collected from a mixture of short and long teacher traces.
Using a common dictionary makes feature activity comparable across both groups.
We then identify candidate features through within-problem comparisons of correct traces and examine whether their associations persist across held-out questions and SAE training seeds.
Token contexts and activation trajectories help characterize whether a feature tracks calculation, explanation, repetition, or answer formatting.
These interpretations remain hypotheses: a feature associated with a token pattern need not implement the reasoning operation suggested by that pattern.
Controls for token identity, sequence position, and activation aggregation address alternative explanations for apparent length associations.

The next step turns feature differences into interventions on teacher generation.
We use SAE decoder directions to strengthen features associated with short traces or suppress features associated with long traces, with explicit limits on the perturbation magnitude.
Paired continuations begin from a shared prefix and independent copies of its attention cache, allowing targeted and control interventions to be compared from the same initial state.
Reading back the SAE activations checks whether an intervention changes the intended feature and quantifies changes to other features.
Unmodified generation, matched random directions, and concise prompting provide complementary controls for the resulting changes in output length and correctness.
These comparisons test whether an identified direction offers useful control over generation beyond its observational association with length.

The final stage evaluates the generated traces as teaching data.
Our primary setting uses a Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct teacher and a fixed Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct student on GSM8K.
The SFT design retains one complete, answer-verified solution per training question and compares intervention-generated data with naturally sampled length-ranked traces.
Student initialization, optimization settings, and question support are held fixed, while equal-example and equal-supervision-token comparisons are reported separately.
Teacher correctness, generation cost, trace length, and downstream student accuracy are distinct outcomes: successful length control alone does not establish better distillation.
The generation framework can also produce larger candidate sets, such as sixteen responses per question, for a subsequent reinforcement-learning extension; that extension requires its own policy and training protocol.

The study is organized around three contributions: a paired analysis of the latent differences between short and long correct reasoning; an SAE intervention framework for testing their influence on teacher generation; and a controlled distillation evaluation linking the resulting traces to student learning.
This progression provides a way to investigate what additional content long solutions contain and whether changing the underlying feature activity improves the usefulness of reasoning supervision.
